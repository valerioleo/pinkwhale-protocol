## Execution, step by step

### 1. Loan execution

The lender signs an order offering money and asking for the collateral. The borrower signs the
mirror image. Both are EIP-712 signatures made in a wallet, off chain, costing nothing.

Then anyone at all, the lender, the borrower, or even a bot, submits both to `executeLoan`,
which hands them to `seaport.matchAdvancedOrders`. One transaction, and either all of it
happens or none of it does.

The repayment terms are passed to `executeLoan` **separately**, as ordinary calldata, because
a Seaport order has nowhere to carry arbitrary data. What it does carry is an arbitrary hash,
`zoneHash`, fixed at the moment the order is signed and free for the zone to define. So the
terms travel alongside, and the order commits to their hash.

Which is why `executeLoan` checks that the two match:

```solidity title="contracts/Pinkwhale/Pinkwhale.sol" {16-21}
// [!include ~/contracts/Pinkwhale/Pinkwhale.sol:assert-terms]
```

Lines 16 to 21 are the pair that matters. Rehash the terms that were handed in, compare
against what each signer committed to, and revert if they differ. Whoever submits the
transaction can rewrite the calldata all they like; they cannot make it hash to something
somebody else signed.

The rest of the function is the same idea applied to the rest of the deal: the collateral must
be directed at Pinkwhale, the borrower cannot ask for a longer term than the lender offered,
and both sides must describe the same repayment item for item.

### 2. The collateral lands in escrow

Seaport does the moving. The principal goes from the lender straight to the borrower, and the
<Pill token="bayc">ape</Pill> goes to Pinkwhale, because that is what the lender's
consideration item says and step 1 made sure of it.

Pinkwhale is now holding collateral, and the borrower is holding money.

### 3. Pinkwhale writes both futures

Still inside the same transaction, Pinkwhale builds two new orders and puts them on Seaport:

```solidity title="contracts/Pinkwhale/Utils/SeaportUtils.sol" {29}
// [!include ~/contracts/Pinkwhale/Utils/SeaportUtils.sol:create-resolution-orders]
```

`seaport.validate` on line 29 is the whole reason this works. It marks an order as valid **on
chain**, which lets a contract be an offerer without ever producing a signature. Pinkwhale has
no private key, and does not need one.

Both orders are built straight from the terms that were checked in step 1, **including any
interest**. The lender's consideration is copied in verbatim, `startAmount` and `endAmount`
included, and Seaport interpolates every item linearly across the order's window. So a lender
who signed "100 now, 110 at expiry" has expressed a rate that accrues by the second; equal
amounts give a flat fee instead. Either way **Pinkwhale contains no interest code at all**.

#### Locking each order to one caller

The other thing decided here is *who* is allowed to fulfil each order, and this is the part
worth slowing down for.

Both orders are `FULL_RESTRICTED`, which means Seaport will not settle them without calling
their zone first:

```solidity title="seaport-core/src/lib/ZoneInteraction.sol"
// OrderType 2-3 requires zone to call or approve via authorizeOrder.
if (
    _isRestrictedAndCallerNotZone(parameters.orderType, parameters.zone)
) {
    // ...encode the `authorizeOrder` call...

    // Perform call and ensure a corresponding magic value was returned.
    _callAndCheckStatus(parameters.zone, orderHash, callData, size);
}
```

That is Seaport's own code, and it is the hinge of the whole design. Any restricted order
hands control to `parameters.zone` before settling, and the zone can refuse.

So Pinkwhale writes a `zoneHash` into each order naming the one address allowed to fulfil it,
then checks it when the call comes back:

```solidity title="contracts/Pinkwhale/PinkwhaleZone.sol" {11}
// [!include ~/contracts/Pinkwhale/PinkwhaleZone.sol:authorize-order]
```

Seaport hands the zone a `ZoneParameters` struct, and two of its fields matter: `zoneHash`,
copied straight off the order, and **`fulfiller`, which is whoever called
`fulfillAdvancedOrder`**. Pinkwhale hashes the caller the same way it built the hash in the
first place and compares. A mismatch is `ZoneHashMismatch`, thrown before a single token has
moved.

No allowlist, no mapping of loans to borrowers, no storage at all. The permission rides along
inside the order, and Seaport is the one that tells the zone who is knocking.

From here the loan can end two ways, and both of them are now sitting on Seaport, waiting.



The two windows never overlap. The default order opens one second after the repayment window
shuts.

### 4. The borrower repays

The repayment order offers the <Pill token="bayc">BAYC #8817</Pill> back and asks for whatever
the loan terms say is owed. The borrower fulfils it like any other Seaport order, and the ape
goes home.

Its `zoneHash` is `keccak256(bytes32(0), borrower)`, so by the mechanism above **only the
borrower** can do this. A stranger who tries to pay the money and take the ape is turned away
by the zone.

### 5. Or the lender claims

If the window closes with the loan unpaid, the default order opens one second later.

It offers the same <Pill token="bayc">BAYC #8817</Pill> and asks for **nothing at all**, so it
costs nothing to fill. That is the entire liquidation engine: no auction, no oracle, no keeper.

Its `zoneHash` is `keccak256(repaymentOrderHash, lender)`, so **only the lender** can fulfil
it. But the hash commits to a second thing: the repayment order it was derived from. The
lender has to hand that hash back in `extraData`, and the zone uses it for one more check:

```solidity title="contracts/Pinkwhale/Utils/SeaportUtils.sol" {5-11}
// [!include ~/contracts/Pinkwhale/Utils/SeaportUtils.sol:assert-order-execution]
```

`getOrderStatus` is Seaport's own record of what has been filled. If the repayment order named
here has been filled, the borrower paid the loan off and the claim is refused with
`UpstreamOrderAlreadyFulfilled`.

This is why repaying makes the default order unfillable. Nothing cancelled it. It is
still a perfectly valid Seaport order. It just can no longer answer the question the zone asks
it.


## The half that isn't on chain

Pinkwhale settles loans. It does not find them. Everything above happens once two signed orders
reach the same transaction, and the protocol has no opinion whatsoever about how they got there.

Seaport doesn't either, and this is the gap worth naming. **A signed order is a JSON object and a
signature, and until someone submits it, it lives nowhere.** Storing orders before they settle is
the one piece of infrastructure a Pinkwhale marketplace cannot avoid building.

Nearly everything after that is already on chain. `LoanExecuted` carries the collateral, the
principal, the repayment and the expiry, indexed by loan, lender and borrower. Seaport emits its
own `OrderValidated` with the **full `OrderParameters`** of both resolution orders, which is
exactly the struct `fulfillAdvancedOrder` wants back. So a client can rebuild every open loan,
and both ways to close it, out of `eth_getLogs`.

### A minimal live demo

The smallest thing that lets a stranger take out a loan end to end:

| Piece | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Contracts on a testnet | `pnpm deploy:sepolia`, already here |
| A faucet | `ERC20Token.mint` and `ERC721Token.mintNext` are unpermissioned. Two buttons, no server |
| A table of signed orders | one row each: parameters, signature, terms. Any KV store does |
| A frontend | signs orders with [`scripts/lib/orders.ts`](https://github.com/valerioleo/pinkwhale-protocol/blob/main/scripts/lib/orders.ts), calls `executeLoan`, reads loans back from logs |

That is the whole list. No indexer, no matching engine, no oracle, no keeper, no server-side
signing. The table is the only stateful thing in it, and every row in it is a signature its
author can cancel on chain or simply replace.

The flow is symmetrical. A borrower signs an order offering collateral and asking for principal;
a lender signs the mirror. Either can go first, because whichever order is already in the table
is enough for the other side to derive its mirror and execute. Whoever moves second pays the gas.

### What a real one adds

- **Spam and staleness.** Signing is free, so anyone can flood the table with orders they cannot
honour. Every row needs its balance, approval and Seaport counter checked before it is shown, and
rechecked when `OrderCancelled` or `CounterIncremented` fires. Nothing on chain announces that a
stored order has gone bad, so the table has to go and look.

- **Search and ranking.** An offer is a duration, a principal and a repayment amount, and none of
those sort usefully on their own. APR comes from `startAmount`, `endAmount` and the window; LTV
needs a floor price. That is where an oracle finally enters this design, and not to liquidate
anything: only to order a list.

- **Standing offers, and the shape they force.** A lender's order names no borrower: its
consideration is collateral addressed to Pinkwhale, and the lender is named only inside the
repayment terms. Point it at `ERC721_WITH_CRITERIA` and it becomes a standing offer against a
whole collection that any borrower can take. But `executeLoan` requires both sides to describe
the same repayment item for item, and the only slack is duration, where a borrower may ask for
less than the lender allowed. **So the book never crosses on price.** A marketplace here is a
listing board where the taker signs the maker's exact terms, and the matching engine is client
code deriving one order from the other.

- **Telling people.** Nothing forces a lender to claim a defaulted loan and nothing warns a
borrower that the window is closing. The expiry is in `LoanExecuted`, so the reminder is a cron
over the logs, but somebody has to run it.
